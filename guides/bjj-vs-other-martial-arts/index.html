Choosing a Martial Art

BJJ vs Judo vs Muay Thai: What's the Difference?

Still deciding which martial art to try? Here's what each one actually trains, in plain terms, so you can pick based on what you actually want out of it.

Written with Fabio Ferrari, Black Belt & Head Coach, Impulse BJJ

  2. BJJ vs Judo

"Which martial art should I do?" doesn't have one right answer, it depends what you're actually training for: control, throws, striking, fitness, or a mix. Here's how Brazilian Jiu Jitsu compares to the three martial arts people ask about most.

BJJ vs traditional Jiu-Jitsu

This is the most confused comparison of the three, largely because of the name. Brazilian Jiu Jitsu developed from Judo and traditional Japanese Jiu-Jitsu in the early 1900s, then evolved into its own distinct art focused almost entirely on ground control, positioning and submissions. Traditional Japanese Jiu-Jitsu is broader: throws, strikes, joint locks and weapons defence, often with far less live sparring. If a school advertises "Jiu-Jitsu" without "Brazilian" in front of it, it's very likely teaching something meaningfully different to what we teach at Impulse.

BJJ vs Judo

Judo and BJJ share a common ancestor, and it shows: both use grips, control and leverage over brute strength. The real difference is where each one spends its time. Judo is built around throws, getting an opponent from standing to the ground decisively, with matches often ending the moment someone hits the mat. BJJ picks up from there: once you're on the ground, BJJ is about controlling position and working toward a submission. Many practitioners train both, since a good throw into a strong ground game is a genuinely complete skill set.

BJJ vs Muay Thai

These two barely overlap, which is exactly why they're often trained together. Muay Thai is a striking art: punches, kicks, knees and elbows, trained standing up. BJJ is grappling: almost entirely about clinching, takedowns and ground control. Neither replaces the other. Someone training purely for MMA will typically need both, which is part of why our No-Gi grappling classes exist alongside a wider MMA-focused approach.

How to actually choose

A few honest rules of thumb: if the idea of throwing strikes doesn't appeal to you, or you want something effective without needing to hit anyone, BJJ's control-based approach (see our self-defence programme) is usually the more comfortable starting point. If you're drawn to explosive, dynamic movement, Judo's throws might appeal more. If you want a full striking outlet, Muay Thai is the more direct route. And if you're not sure yet, that's fine too, come try a free class and decide from the mat rather than from a search result.

Quick answers

Is Brazilian Jiu Jitsu the same as Jiu-Jitsu?

No. Brazilian Jiu Jitsu developed from Judo and traditional Japanese Jiu-Jitsu in the early 1900s, and evolved into its own distinct ground-grappling art. Traditional Japanese Jiu-Jitsu includes a much wider mix of throws, strikes and weapons defence, whereas BJJ is almost entirely focused on ground control and submissions.

Is BJJ better than Judo for self-defence?

Neither is simply better. Judo excels at getting a bigger or stronger opponent to the ground quickly through throws. BJJ excels once you're already there, controlling and neutralising them. Many practitioners see the two as complementary rather than competing.

Can I train BJJ and Muay Thai at the same time?

Yes, and it's a common combination. BJJ and Muay Thai cover almost entirely different ranges of a fight (ground grappling versus striking), so training both builds a much more complete skill set, which is exactly why most MMA gyms teach both.
